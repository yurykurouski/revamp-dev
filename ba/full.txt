Introduction
Relevance and problem
Small local businesses such as clinics, restaurants, repair shops and beauty salons rely on their
websites to be found and contacted, yet many of these sites are slow, hard to use and visibly
dated. Web-wide measurements show how common such defects are: in 2026 automated tests
detected WCAG 2 accessibility failures on 95.9% of the one million most popular home pages,
with an average of 56.1 errors per page [1], and in 2024 only 43% of websites had good Core
Web Vitals on mobile devices [2]. These figures describe popular sites in general, not local
businesses specifically.
For web development studios and freelancers (called studios below) these businesses are natural
clients, but reaching them is expensive. A convincing first contact requires researching the
business, assessing its website and preparing a personalised proposal, ideally with a visual
example of a better site, and this work is lost when the business does not reply. Studios also
draw on the same public sources, such as map services and business directories, so the pool of
prospects in a city is finite and shared: one business can receive several competing pitches.
The question is timely because multimodal large language models (LLMs) can now turn a
screenshot of a web page into front-end code; a NAACL 2025 benchmark of 484 real web pages
found that their main weaknesses are missing visual elements and incorrect layouts [3]. A draft
prototype can therefore be prepared for every prospect at low cost, provided that the output is
checked. The same technology sets the limit of automation: language models produce fluent
content that their input does not support [4], so metrics, contact details and facts in an offer
cannot be left to a model, and sending generated e-mails without human review adds legal and
reputational risk.
The problem is therefore twofold: the first contact with a local business costs a studio too much
manual work, and the shared pool of businesses is contested without rules, while full automation
with LLMs alone is unreliable. Revamp addresses it with a multi-tenant SaaS platform that
audits each business website once with deterministic tools and publishes masked listings. A
studio buys the exclusive right to contact a business for a fixed period at a price derived from
the audit, receives an LLM-generated MVP prototype of the improved site, and sends the offer
from its own address after its operator approves every message.

Goal and objectives
The goal of the project is to develop a multi-tenant SaaS platform that cuts the effort a web
studio spends to reach a qualified local-business client by combining deterministic website
audits, LLM-generated MVP prototypes and fixed-price purchase of exclusive contact rights,
with human approval of every message. Its objectives are:
1. Analyse how web studios find clients today, review the literature and existing tools, and
identify the gaps (Chapter 1).
2. Define the vision, measurable KPIs, stakeholders, scope and solution concept of the
platform (sections 2.1–2.4).
3. Specify the functional requirements as epics E1–E5 and prioritised user stories with
Given/When/Then acceptance criteria traced to the KPIs (sections 2.5–2.6).
4. Specify the non-functional and legal requirements (GDPR, ePrivacy, WCAG 2.1 AA)
and the project risks (sections 2.7–2.9).
5. Define how the result is verified against the KPIs: automated tests and a market
simulation of the pricing rule (section 2.10).
Object and subject
The object of the project is the process by which web development studios and freelancers
acquire new clients among small local businesses. The subject is the part of this process that
can be automated: finding businesses, auditing their websites, allocating the right to contact a
business between studios, preparing a personalised prototype and offer, and sending outreach
that a human has approved.
Methods
The requirements were gathered with document analysis of legal texts and standards (GDPR,
ePrivacy rules, WCAG 2.1) and of the project’s concept and decision log; a literature review
(section 1.2); a comparative analysis of existing lead generation and website audit tools
(section 1.3); an analysis of the existing prototype, a working single-operator Revamp
pipeline (discovery, audit, MVP generation, review and e-mail) that gives the KPI baselines;
and modelling with UML diagrams and a planned market simulation of pricing rules. No
interviews or surveys with studios or business owners were conducted: the personas in section
2.2 are constructed, and statements about studio behaviour without a source are marked as
assumptions.

Chapter 1. Analysis of the Subject Area
1.1 Current State of the Process
Studios and freelancers win clients through referrals, through directories where businesses
search for them, such as Clutch (section 1.3), and through their own outbound search. This
section describes the outbound path, the one Revamp automates. The description is the author’s
model, built from the project concept, the analogs in section 1.3 and the existing Revamp
prototype. It was not validated with studios, because no interviews were held, so the duration
and frequency of each step are assumptions to be measured in a pilot.
Three parties take part (Fig. 1). A person at the studio (the owner of a small studio or a
freelancer) chooses a niche and an area, finds local businesses in map services and directories,
opens each website and judges its quality, looks for contact details, checks the studio’s own
records to avoid a second pitch, prepares a proposal with a mock-up and sends a personal email. The business owner receives the e-mail and either replies or ignores it and asks to stop.
The tools involved are public map services and directories, website audit tools, the studio’s
spreadsheet or CRM, a design tool and an e-mail client.

is neither repeatable nor reusable, and it cannot justify a price.

•

L2, contact history. Each studio checks only its own records. No studio knows whether
another one has already pitched the business, and an opt-out reaches only the studio that
received it.

•

L3, proposal and mock-up. The work is done by hand for each prospect before the
studio knows whether the business is interested, and it is lost if there is no reply.

•

L4, repeated pitches. Because studios search the same public sources, one business can
receive pitches from several studios and has no single way to stop them.

Every step up to the e-mail is manual and is repeated for each business, including those that
never reply, so the studio’s time grows with the number of businesses it checks rather than with
the clients it wins. How long a lead takes was not measured (assumption A3). A general survey
of sales professionals points the same way: they report spending 70% of their time on tasks
other than selling [6]. It does not describe web studios and is used here as context only.
TO-BE. With Revamp the platform searches an area and audits each website once with
deterministic tools, so the evidence is repeatable and shared (L1). Studios browse masked
listings and buy the exclusive right to contact a business for a fixed period at a price derived
from the audit; a contact registry across all studios blocks repeated pitches and applies every
opt-out (L2, L4). The prototype is generated only for the buyer, and the studio’s operator
reviews it and approves the e-mail, which is sent from the studio’s own address (L3). A business
owner can also audit their own site on a public page and consent to be contacted.

1.2 Review of Sources
The sources were chosen to answer four questions that shape the requirements: how good the
websites of potential clients are and how this can be measured; how far LLMs can be trusted to
prepare an offer; what the law requires from B2B outreach; and how a market for leads should
be priced. They include standards and legal texts, peer-reviewed papers and industry reports.
Industry reports are used for context only, never as the basis of a KPI.
Website quality and its measurement
Industry measurements show that accessibility and performance defects are common on the
web [1], [2], and Google’s case studies report business gains after Core Web Vitals were
improved, for example 8% more sales after a 31% improvement of Largest Contentful Paint
at Vodafone Italy [7]. These case studies are published by the author of the metric and describe
large companies, so Revamp may use them to explain an offer but never to promise a result.
WCAG 2.1 [8] gives a shared vocabulary for accessibility defects and three conformance levels
(A, AA and AAA). Automated checking of it has clear limits: Vigo et al. [9] found that six
automated tools covered at most 50% of the WCAG 2.0 success criteria and caught 14% to
6

38% of the violations, and the more complete tools produced more false positives. Two
requirements follow. The audit reports violations detected by automated rules and never states
that a site does or does not conform to WCAG. And because the audit result sets the price, it
must be repeatable rather than complete: the same site must give the same result (KPI in section
2.1, determinism requirement in section 2.7).
Reliability of LLM-generated content
Si et al. [3] show that multimodal models can produce a web page from a screenshot, but that
they miss visual elements and get layouts wrong. Ji et al. [4] describe hallucination, content that
is not supported by the source, as a general problem of neural text generation. For Revamp this
means that a model may design and write the prototype, but code must check the page before
anyone sees it and must keep every fact: metrics, contact details, prices and names come only
from deterministic extraction. The operator reviews the prototype and approves every e-mail
(US3; the grounding and human-in-the-loop requirements in section 2.7; the share of generated
pages that pass the code checks as a KPI in section 2.1).
Legal rules for B2B outreach
The GDPR [10] applies whenever a business contact identifies a natural person, for example
the e-mail address of a sole trader. Recital 47 states that processing for direct marketing may
be regarded as carried out for a legitimate interest, so Article 6(1)(f) can be the legal basis for
outbound contact after a balancing test. Article 21(2)–(3) gives the person the right to object at
any time, after which the data may no longer be processed for marketing; Article 5(1)(c)
requires data minimisation and Article 25 data protection by design. The ePrivacy Directive
[11] requires prior consent for marketing e-mail to natural persons (Article 13(1)), apart from
similar products offered to existing customers, leaves the protection of legal persons to national
law (Article 13(5)), and prohibits marketing e-mail that conceals the sender or has no valid
address for opting out (Article 13(4)). The platform therefore stores only business contacts
published by the business, applies every opt-out across all studios (US4), shows the sending
studio’s identity in each message, needs a legal review for each target country, and offers a
consent-based inbound flow (US8) as the lawful channel where prior consent is required
(section 2.8).
Pricing a market for leads
Rochet and Tirole [12] model platforms that must attract both sides of a market and analyse
what determines how prices are allocated between the sides. Revamp has two such sides:
7

studios pay for contact rights, while business owners pay nothing and are protected by the
contact registry and offered a free public audit (US4, US8). Einav et al. [13] explain why eBay
sellers moved from auctions to posted prices as a trade-off between competitive price discovery
and convenience, driven mainly by falling demand for auctions. This supports selling contact
rights at a fixed, published price derived from the audit (US5) and deferring the auction until
the platform has enough studios (section 2.4).
Overall, the sources confirm the need for the product and set three limits on it: automated audits
are partial, LLM output must be checked, and outreach must respect objections and, in some
countries, prior consent. Table 1 links each source to the requirements it informs.

1.3 Analysis of Analogs
Three existing solutions were compared, one from each group that covers part of the studio’s
task: a commercial B2B contact database with outreach automation (Apollo.io), an open-source
website audit tool (Lighthouse with its hosted front end PageSpeed Insights), and a commercial
marketplace where businesses look for web development companies (Clutch). Each fact in the
8

comparison comes from the vendor’s own pages or from the author’s hands-on trials in October
2026, and a property the sources do not state is marked "not stated". A fourth candidate, the
SEOptimer embeddable audit widget for agencies, was dropped because its website blocked
automated access with a bot check, so its features could not be verified. Table 2 summarises
the comparison.
Apollo.io (commercial, tried)
Apollo.io presents itself as a single platform for B2B data, AI agents and outreach and
advertises a database of more than 240 million contacts and 30 million companies [14]. It offers
Free, Basic, Professional and Custom plans with credits shared by the team; the pricing page
lists no amounts, shows a "GDPR Compliant" label and says nothing about exclusive contacts
[15]. Outreach runs as sequences whose steps can be automatic or manual e-mails [16], so
messages can be sent without a person approving each one. The author tried Apollo with a
personal account. It offers a very broad set of functions for finding leads, but none of them is
focused on the quality of a prospect’s website. The website data it does offer is visitor tracking:
the Apollo Script is installed on the customer’s own websites and shows which companies visit
them [17], so it describes the studio’s own site, not the sites of its prospects. Apollo is therefore
a source of contacts rather than an alternative to an offer built on a website audit.
Lighthouse and PageSpeed Insights (open source, tried)
Lighthouse is an open-source tool under the Apache-2.0 license that audits a page for
performance, accessibility, best practices and SEO and can run from Chrome DevTools, the
command line or a Node module [18]; PageSpeed Insights runs it in a simulated environment
[5]. It was tried on the W3C "Before and After" demonstration page, a site built to be
inaccessible: the mobile report gave Performance 100, Accessibility 50, Best Practices 88 and
SEO 75 (Fig. 6 in Appendix B). Two observations matter for Revamp. First, a visibly dated
page scored 100 for performance, so speed alone does not show that a site needs a redesign.
Second, Google states that lab scores can change between runs even when the page has not
changed [5], so a single run is a weak basis for a price. Lighthouse finds no leads and sends
nothing.
Clutch (commercial, tried without an account)
Clutch is a directory where business buyers look for service providers; its web development
category listed 99,797 companies, sorted by "Sponsored" by default, with the note "We may
earn a fee for some placements" [19] (Fig. 7 in Appendix B). The directory was browsed without
9

an account. It works in the opposite direction to outbound lead generation: the business owner
must already know that the site needs work and must start the search, and studios pay for
visibility to all buyers, not for access to a particular business.

What is missing
None of the compared solutions links the three parts of the studio’s task. Apollo supplies
contacts and automates sending, but gives no evidence about the business’s website, no
exclusivity and no mandatory human approval. Lighthouse gives such evidence, but only for a
URL the user already has, with no lead, no offer and results that can vary between runs. Clutch
connects businesses with studios, but only when the business starts the search, and it sells
visibility rather than access to a specific business. Revamp is meant to fill this gap with four
properties that become the comparison criteria on the defense slide and the input to section 1.4:
a repeatable deterministic audit as the evidence for each lead, an LLM-generated prototype of
the improved site, an exclusive and fairly priced right to contact the business, and human
approval of every message with an opt-out that applies to all studios.

Chapter 2. Requirements for the Revamp Platform
2.1 Vision and Business Goals
Vision. Revamp gives web studios ready, evidence-backed leads. It audits the websites of local
businesses once with deterministic tools, builds an LLM-generated prototype of a better site
from the business’s own content, and sells each studio the exclusive right to contact a business
at a fixed price, with a human approving every message.
The business goals follow from the problems in section 1.4:
•

G1. Cut the time a studio spends on each reviewed lead, while keeping a human
approval before every message (P1).

•

G2. Replace the contested pool of businesses with a fair market: one studio at a time
holds the right to contact a business, at a transparent price (P4).

•

G3. Keep every metric and contact detail deterministic and verifiable, and check every
generated page by code (P2, P3).

•

G4. Serve many studios on one platform with strict data isolation, and let business
owners audit their own site and consent to be contacted (P5).

The system has four kinds of users: studio owners, who manage the organization, its members
and its budget; studio operators, who browse listings, buy rights, review prototypes and approve
e-mails (a viewer role can read but not approve or send); business owners, who use the public

and prices and runs the market simulation. Two constructed personas summarise the studio
side. They are derived from the problem analysis in Chapter 1, not from interviews with real
people.
Persona A, the solo freelancer. Builds sites for local businesses in one city and does outreach
alone after client work. Needs a few qualified leads a week with evidence to show the owner,
cannot afford unanswered mock-ups, and fears complaints that would hurt their own domain.
Key stories: buy a contact right (US5), browse masked listings (US10), contact only businesses
they hold the right for (US3).
Persona B, the small studio owner. Runs a few developers and one person who handles
outreach. Needs a shared workspace with roles, control of spending on leads, and certainty that
two members never pitch the same business. Key stories: create a studio organization (US1),
keep the studio’s data isolated (US2), save interests in a niche and an area (US11).

In scope. The existing core pipeline E0 (discovery, audit, MVP generation with code checks,

2.4 Solution Concept
Revamp is a multi-tenant SaaS platform; each studio is a tenant. A studio saves private interests
(a niche and an area). An interest is only a demand signal: it gives no right and no priority, and
other studios never see it. When there is enough demand, or when the platform operator
schedules it, the platform scans the area, audits each matching website once and publishes
masked listings at published release times. A listing shows the niche, the district, the audit
result and the price, but not the business name, domain, screenshots or contacts; a listing that
fewer than k similar businesses share is withheld so that a profile cannot single out a company.

2.8 Legal and Ethical Requirements
Personal data. The platform processes business contact details that the business itself publishes
(e-mail address, phone number, address), which identify a natural person in the case of a sole
trader; the account data of studio users; and consent records of the inbound flow. It processes
no data about private individuals. The legal basis for outbound B2B contact is legitimate interest
under Article 6(1)(f) GDPR, supported by Recital 47 and a balancing test, with the right to
object under Article 21 applied across all studios [10]. The inbound flow relies on consent,
19

which is stored with its time and text and can be withdrawn; studio accounts and payments rely
on the contract. Because Article 13 of the ePrivacy Directive leaves the rules for legal persons
to national law [11], each target country needs a legal review before outreach starts there. The
roles of the platform and the studios as controllers, retention periods and the handling of access
and deletion requests will be fixed in a Privacy Notice and a Data Processing Agreement (task
REV-176).
Participants. No interviews or surveys were carried out, so no participant consent was needed;
Lighthouse and Clutch were tried on public pages without accounts, and Apollo with the
author’s own account.
Licenses. The main dependencies are open source: Playwright (Apache-2.0), axe-core
(MPL-2.0), Express, Mongoose, BullMQ, React and Material UI (MIT). OpenStreetMap data
is available under the Open Database License and requires attribution [20]; Google Places data
is used under Google’s terms.
Standards and ethics. The dashboard and the public audit page meet WCAG 2.1 AA [8]
(NFR8). Audit reports never claim that a site conforms to WCAG, because automated checks
cover only part of it [9]. Every e-mail names the sending studio and carries an opt-out; masked
listings with the k threshold prevent profiling of a single business.

2.10 Conclusions to Chapter 2
The specification fixes the vision, four business goals and KPIs K1–K4 with their baselines and
targets, the scope with its assumptions and constraints, the solution concept with the rejected
alternatives, 17 user stories in six epics (14 Must stories with Given/When/Then criteria), two
use cases, ten non-functional requirements, the legal basis of processing and seven risks. The
result will be verified as follows:
•

K1: the median operator time per reviewed lead is at most 15 minutes in a pilot,
compared with a baseline measured on the manual process first.

•

K2: at least 95% of at least 20 audited sites get the same band and price on two runs a
day apart.

•

K3: at least 80% of at least 30 generated pages pass the code checks on the first attempt.

•

K4: the contact registry shows no business contacted by two studios within the
cooldown, in the pilot and in the simulation.

•

Stories and NFRs: every Given/When/Then criterion becomes an automated
acceptance test, and NFR1–NFR10 are checked by load, isolation and accessibility tests.
The market simulation (US7) compares revenue, unsold share, buyer concentration and
overpayment for each pricing rule.

US10

As a studio operator, I want to filter
open lots by niche, area and audit
band, so that I can choose which
businesses to buy without seeing who
they are.

Given an open lot, when any studio requests the listing,
then only masked fields are returned: niche, district, audit
band, price and release time.
Given a profile that fewer than k businesses share, when
listings are published, then that listing is withheld.
Given one studio’s listing requests, when they exceed the
rate limit, then further requests are rejected.

Must

US11

As a studio operator, I want to save an
interest in a niche and an area, so that
I am notified about new lots there and
the platform knows where studios
want leads.

Given an interest saved by studio A, when studio B lists
interests, then studio A’s interest is not shown.
Given a saved interest, when matching lots are released,
then the studio is notified and gets no priority over other
studios.

Must

US12

As the platform operator, I want to
scan an area when demand reaches a
threshold and to release lots at
published times, so that audit cost
follows demand and every studio has
the same chance.

Given demand below the threshold and no scheduled scan,
when the scheduler runs, then no scan is queued.
Given a lot with a published release time, when that time
comes, then every studio can buy it.
Given a business that is already audited, when its area is
scanned again, then the existing audit is reused.

Must

Given an owner without an account, when they enter their
site address, then they get the audit result.
Given an audit result, when the owner gives explicit
consent, then a lead is created and the consent is stored
with its time and text.
Given a consented lead, when the owner withdraws
consent, then the right ends, queued e-mails are cancelled
and the unused part of the payment is refunded.

Should

Given a map cell with too few purchases, when the map is
shown, then the cell shows no price and never reveals a
buyer.
